\begin{abstract}
We report that two decisions usually treated as implementation detail --- how often
a monitoring system decides, and how its features are normalised --- each outweigh
the choice of classifier on cross-machine pump fault diagnosis.

Our two-tier system for irrigation pumps initially achieved 0.168 end-to-end fault
recall at a budget of one false alarm per pump per month: roughly one fault in
six, and not deployable. The cause was not the model. A duty cycle of twelve
feature windows per runtime hour puts $\sim$1080 decisions in a pump-month, so a
single tolerated alarm demands 99.907\,\% specificity per decision. Holding the
operator-facing promise \emph{fixed} at one alarm per month and deciding once per
runtime day instead demands 96.7\,\%, and raises end-to-end recall to
\textbf{0.647} --- a $3.9\times$ improvement on identical data and features, at no
cost in energy (battery life unchanged at 1.2 years) and none in latency for the
slowly developing faults this tier exists to catch. \textbf{The operating point
also decides which model wins}: a prior-fitted tabular foundation model leads by
$2.4\times$ at the original cadence and is overtaken by a gradient-boosted baseline
at every deployable one, so a comparison of the two is not a statement about the
models unless the operating point accompanies it.

Separately, on the same eleven pumps, standardising each machine by its own
statistics costs 0.20--0.28 macro-F1 against pooling the training machines, on 11
of 11 machines, where the margin between models is 0.072. The effect replicates on
an independent bearing benchmark. We do \emph{not} establish its mechanism: our
class-imbalance explanation is refuted by our own controlled test.

We also report a five-rung leakage ladder under which random-window splits nearly
double reported macro-F1 on real machines, and the finding that the gate, not the
classifier, bounds the system: on the worst pump its recall ceiling is 0.52.

Throughout we treat protocol as the object of study rather than a preliminary, and
we report the two occasions on which our own protocol was the thing at fault ---
including one where we mistook a broken commissioning procedure for a property of
industrial machinery. From those we derive a four-point gate protocol, of which the
first point is that a condition monitor must establish whether the machine is
running before it evaluates anything.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ~500 words. Keep the two-timescale framing — it motivates the architecture.
An irrigation pump fails on two very different timescales, and conflating them is
the design error this work is organised around.

The failure that destroys equipment fastest is dry running. When suction is lost
the mechanical seal loses the fluid film that lubricates and cools it, and the
faces are damaged within tens of seconds. No architecture that ships a
measurement to a server and waits for a reply can prevent this. Dry running is
therefore not a classification problem in our design; it is a local trip.

The failures that dominate lifetime cost are slower: bearing wear, impeller
damage, misalignment, cavitation, a loosening foot. These develop over days to
months and are worth diagnosing rather than merely detecting, because the
maintenance action differs by fault. Here latency is irrelevant and
discrimination is everything.

The obstacle to machine learning at this price point is not inference cost but
\emph{commissioning} cost. A per-pump supervised model needs labelled examples of
each fault on each pump, which for a population of smallholder pumps costs more
than the pumps. We attack this with a prior-fitted tabular model that conditions
on a reference set supplied at inference time rather than fitting weights to it.
Commissioning becomes substituting a reference set, not retraining.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(C1) \textbf{The operating point dominates the model.} Holding the operator-facing
promise of one false alarm per pump per month fixed and deciding once per runtime
day instead of every five minutes raises end-to-end recall from 0.168 to
\textbf{0.647} --- $3.9\times$, at no cost in energy or in latency that matters. It
also \emph{inverts which model is best}. We examine the method critically in
\S\ref{sec:opmethod}, including a naive form that buys nothing and a stronger form
we could not test.
(C2) \textbf{Normalisation outweighs model choice}: 0.20--0.28 macro-F1 on eleven
in-service pumps against a 0.072 margin between models, on 11 of 11 machines,
replicated on an independent bearing benchmark. The mechanism is \emph{not}
established, and we refute our own first explanation.
(C3) A five-rung leakage ladder quantifying how much reported accuracy is
manufactured by the split, and the observation that one widely used pump dataset
cannot support cross-machine evaluation at all.
(C4) A two-tier architecture with gateway latency measured on the deployment board,
and the finding that the gate, not the classifier, bounds end-to-end recall.

We also report negative results that revise our own design: vibration is the wrong
primary sensor for dry running, ``training-free'' overstates what in-context
learning provides, and the edge accelerators on our deployment board cannot take a
model whose input shape varies by construction.

\section{Related Work}
%% ~450 words. The positioning against [6] is the load-bearing part — do not cut it.
Prior-data fitted networks invert the usual training relationship: a transformer
is pre-trained once on synthetic tabular tasks, then conditions on a labelled
reference set at inference time without gradient updates. Hollmann
\emph{et al.}~\cite{hollmann2025} show this outperforms tuned gradient-boosted
trees on datasets up to roughly ten thousand rows --- the regime a per-pump
commissioning set occupies. The property we need is not accuracy at small $n$ but
that adapting to a new machine is a data substitution rather than a training run.

The closest precedent for our classifier is Magad\'an \emph{et al.}~\cite{magadan2023},
who apply this model family to early fault classification in rotating machinery
under limited data. Three things separate our work: they evaluate the first
version of the model; their machines are motors and bearings rather than pumps,
so the taxonomy excludes cavitation, impeller damage and dry running; and their
evaluation is offline, with no deployment target, energy budget or gating tier.

Our protocol contribution sits in a small but growing literature arguing that
reported accuracy in vibration-based diagnosis is substantially an artefact of
how data are split. Varej\~ao \emph{et al.}~\cite{varejao2024} name the
phenomenon \emph{similarity bias} --- chunks of one chopped signal appearing on
both sides of the split --- and on the pump data we use report their best model
falling from an F-measure of 0.887 to 0.733 once their sampling strategy removes
it. Wheat \emph{et al.}~\cite{wheat2024} reach a compatible conclusion on bearing
data, distinguishing segmentation-level from bearing-level leakage.

Closest to our protocol contribution is Vieira \emph{et al.}~\cite{vieira2025},
who survey eighteen 2025 papers, find leakage persists in the majority, and
propose a methodology built on \emph{bearing-wise} partitioning across four
benchmarks. Their taxonomy and ours agree closely: their segment-wise and
repetition-wise splits are our levels 0--1, condition-wise is our level 3, and
bearing-wise --- the strictest they define --- is our level 2. The scenario they
motivate it with is a bearing replaced during maintenance on the same machine.

Our contribution is the rung above. Leave-one-machine-out holds out an entire
in-service pump: different installation, duty cycle and commissioning history,
not a swapped component on a shared rig. That is what a node deployed on a
customer's pump faces, and it is unavailable in the bearing benchmarks that
literature uses, because their components share a test rig. Vieira
\emph{et al.} also corroborate our central limitation independently, finding that
the number of unique training components decides robust performance --- the same
conclusion we reach about machines, on different data with a different model
family.

\section{System Design}
%% ~600 words + Fig. 1. The energy inversion is the most quotable result here.
\subsection{Two tiers, split by timescale}
The node is a battery-powered microcontroller with an accelerometer and a current
transformer. It runs a continuous low-cost statistic over the sensor stream,
trips the contactor locally on dry running, and escalates anomalous windows to
the gateway. It never classifies. The gateway is an ARM-class single-board
computer shared across a site; it holds the reference set and classifies
escalated feature windows. This split is a latency partition, not a capability
partition.

\subsection{The dry-run trip}
The trip requires three conditions \emph{simultaneously}: a CUSUM detector past
threshold, an absolute level below a floor, and persistence over a dwell period.
The conjunction is load-bearing. An earlier version disjoined the floor and the
CUSUM decision and tripped on essentially every startup transient --- a pump that
has not yet primed looks exactly like one that has lost suction for the few
seconds before it fills.

\textbf{Negative result.} The design began with a vibration-driven trip.
Vibration energy \emph{falls} when a pump loses suction, which is the opposite of
the signature a threshold detector wants, while motor current drops immediately
and unambiguously. We report this because the vibration-first design is the
intuitive one and it is wrong.

\subsection{The gate, and what actually costs energy}
The gate is commissioned on \emph{each pump's own healthy baseline}: a Mahalanobis
distance in the gate feature space plus an EWMA on level, with thresholds from
that pump's own healthy windows. This matches deployment --- a node is installed
on a presumed-healthy pump, observes it, and escalates what does not look like
what it saw. Commissioning requires $n > 10p$ healthy windows; estimated from
fewer, the covariance is degenerate and the distance rejects everything.

\textbf{The energy result inverts our starting assumption.} The design originally
budgeted radio transmission at roughly 55\,\% of the node's energy and optimised
the payload accordingly. That figure was computed for a \emph{fixed} transmission
schedule, which the same design had already rejected in favour of event-triggered
uplink. With a working gate, measured transmission is about 1\,\% of the node
budget and continuous sensing is essentially all of the rest. The optimisation
target is a cheaper always-on front end, not a smaller payload.

\subsection{Gateway inference}
The reference set is fixed between commissioning events, so the transformer's
key/value state can be cached once at boot rather than re-encoding the context on
every query. Measured on the deployment board --- a Rockchip RK3588, four
Cortex-A76 and four Cortex-A55 cores, single-threaded --- this is worth
$7.1\times$, and reducing the ensemble from eight members to one a further
$5.6\times$. Both ratios reproduce the values we measured on a development
workstation ($7.4\times$, $5.5\times$) to within 0.3, on hardware roughly
$21\times$ slower; the board is uniformly $21$--$22\times$ slower across all four
configurations with no configuration-dependent cliff, indicating a compute-bound
workload. In the deployed configuration a classification costs 88\,ms against two
to three escalations per pump per day, so latency is nowhere near binding.

\textbf{Negative result: no accelerator fits.} We did not attempt a port and do
not claim impossibility. Both the on-board NPU and an Edge TPU require a
fixed-shape, fully-INT8 graph from a restricted operator set. The reference set is
part of the model's input, so the input tensor has shape
$(n_\text{context}+n_\text{query}, n_\text{features})$ and varies with
commissioning. Padding would fix the shape but not the two obstacles that matter:
the operator sets do not cover a transformer attention stack, and INT8 quantising
a prior-fitted model without degrading the calibration our abstention depends on
is an open problem. The gateway is shared and mains-powered, so CPU inference
suffices.

\textbf{Negative result: ``training-free'' overstates it.} In-context learning
removes per-pump \emph{gradient} training. It does not remove the reference set,
the commissioning procedure, the feature pipeline, the per-pump gate thresholds
or the normalisation statistics.

\section{Datasets and Evaluation Protocol}
%% ~550 words + Table I. The Twente LOMO impossibility is a strong, citable claim.
\subsection{Two complementary real datasets}
No single public dataset supports this evaluation, so we use two that fail in
opposite directions (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). ESPset~\cite{varejao2024} is
eleven in-service electrical submersible pumps with published order-normalised
velocity spectra, and is the only source of a genuine cross-machine axis. The
Twente/4TU dataset~\cite{kumar2023} is a laboratory rig: two motors at four
speeds, with both vibration and motor current.

We state a scope limitation plainly: ESPset pumps are offshore submersibles, not
smallholder irrigation pumps. They share the failure physics and nothing else,
including duty cycle, size and installation. The cross-machine result is evidence
about the method, not about irrigation pumps specifically.

\textbf{Twente cannot support leave-one-machine-out at all.} Its two motors share
only the healthy class: every fault present on one is absent from the other. Such
a fold would train on one label set and test on a disjoint one, producing a number
that looks like a cross-machine result and means nothing. Our splitter checks this
condition --- every fold must train on the classes it tests --- and marks folds
that fail it as uninterpretable rather than scoring them.

\subsection{The leakage ladder}
Our central protocol claim is that in machinery fault diagnosis the split, not the
model, determines most of the reported accuracy. We evaluate every model at five
levels of increasing strictness on identical features:
(0)~\emph{random-window} --- windows shuffled regardless of origin; \textbf{invalid},
since windows from one recording appear on both sides;
(1)~\emph{record-wise} --- no recording spans the split;
(2)~\emph{component-wise} --- no physical component instance spans the split;
(3)~\emph{cross-operating} --- train and test at different operating points;
(4)~\emph{leave-one-machine-out} --- the held-out machine is never seen.
Level 0 is reported not as a baseline but as an artefact, to quantify what common
practice buys.

\subsection{Normalisation is protocol, not preprocessing}
Per-machine normalisation is standard and quietly changes what is measured. We
evaluate two explicit strategies: \emph{per-machine}, where each machine is
standardised by its own statistics including the held-out one (transductive, and
legitimate when a node self-commissions on the target pump); and
\emph{train-pooled}, using training-machine statistics only (inductive, the
stricter reading). We report both and label every table, because on these data
the gap reaches 0.25 macro-F1 --- larger than the effect of the model choice.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The operating point dominates the model}
\label{sec:op}
Our system's first honest end-to-end number was \textbf{0.168}: the gate discards
some faults, and the best classifier at the design's false-alarm budget recovers
0.203 of what reaches it. One fault in six is not a detector. The cause is
arithmetic, not modelling.

The budget of 0.00093 is not a physical constant. It is the operator-facing promise
--- one tolerated false alarm per pump per month --- divided by the number of
decisions made in that month. Twelve feature windows per runtime hour over three
runtime hours a day puts $\sim$1080 decisions in a pump-month, so a single tolerated
alarm demands \textbf{99.907\,\% specificity per decision}. Deciding less often
divides the same promise across fewer decisions (Table~\ref{tab:cadence}).

Adopting one decision per runtime day takes end-to-end recall from \textbf{0.168 to
0.647} --- a $3.9\times$ improvement on identical data and features, applying the
gate ceiling of 0.83 in both cases. It costs nothing in energy: continuous CUSUM
sensing dominates the node budget, so battery life is unchanged at 1.2 years while
uplinks fall from 2.12 to 0.06 per day. It costs nothing in detection latency for
faults that develop over weeks.

\textbf{The operating point decides which model wins.} This is the sharpest form of
the claim, and we did not anticipate it. At the original cadence the foundation
model is best by a factor of 2.4 over the gradient-boosted baseline (0.203 against
0.084). Somewhere between fifteen minutes and one hour the ranking inverts, and at
every deployable cadence the gradient-boosted baseline is ahead (0.781 against 0.751
at daily). A comparison of these two models is therefore not a statement about the
models at all unless the operating point is given with it. The foundation model's
advantage is specifically an advantage in the extreme-specificity regime --- which
is exactly the regime the original design had accidentally placed itself in, and
exactly the regime a deployable system should leave.

\subsection{Normalisation outweighs model choice}
\label{sec:norm}
Per-machine normalisation is standard in this literature and is usually described
as preprocessing. It is not: it is the largest single effect we measure.

Table~\ref{tab:norm} gives both strategies on eleven in-service pumps under
leave-one-machine-out. Pooling the training machines beats standardising each
machine by its own statistics by 0.20--0.28 macro-F1, on \emph{every} machine
without exception. The margin between our best and worst models on the same data
is 0.072. \textbf{The result is also counterintuitive}: the losing strategy uses
strictly more information about the test machine, since it may use that machine's
own unlabelled statistics.

\textbf{External validation.} The pump result rests on one dataset, because
ESPset is the only public source with a genuine cross-machine axis for pumps. We
therefore tested whether the effect appears at all on independent data: the
Paderborn bearing benchmark~\cite{lessmeier2016}, a different laboratory, machine
type and sensor suite, organised one file per physical bearing. Holding out one
bearing of each class per fold, pooling again wins --- logistic $0.167 \to 0.688$,
gradient-boosted $0.488 \to 0.622$. Under per-unit normalisation logistic
regression lands \emph{exactly} on the majority-class floor of 0.167, meaning the
transform has destroyed all linearly accessible class information.

\textbf{We do not know why, and we say so.} We hypothesised that per-unit
standardisation centres a unit on a mean sitting inside its dominant class, so
that class imbalance within units drives the damage; the correlation supports it
($r=+0.64$ across eleven pumps, permutation $p=0.032$). A controlled test
refutes it: subsampling every machine to equal class counts does not shrink the
gap but widens it (logistic $+0.078 \to +0.184$). A second account --- that
absolute signal level is informative across machines and per-unit standardisation
removes it --- is more promising, taking the gradient-boosted gap from $+0.155$ to
$+0.057$ when level-carrying features are dropped, but the feature partition
available to us is crude and the gap does not close. We report a robust,
replicated effect with the mechanism unresolved, rather than a tidy explanation
we cannot support.

\subsection{Does the expensive model earn its cost?}
Accuracy at a free choice of threshold is not the operational quantity; the
operational quantity is how much fault recall survives at an alarm rate an
operator tolerates. At one false alarm per pump per month --- 1080 decisions per
pump-month, a false alarm rate of $0.00093$ --- recall separates far more sharply
than macro-F1: majority 0.000, logistic 0.032, gradient-boosted 0.084, ours
\textbf{0.203}. The expensive model recovers $2.4\times$ as many faults as the
tuned baseline at the operating point that determines whether the system is
switched off. All pairwise differences are significant under an exact McNemar
test~\cite{dietterich1998}.

\textbf{Commissioning specification.} Sweeping reference-set size, accuracy
saturates near 500 labelled windows (macro-F1 0.739, 0.77\,s per query, against
0.712 at 250 and 0.672 at 100) and \emph{regresses} at 1000 (0.719, 1.22\,s). A
larger reference set is not simply better: past saturation it costs latency and
returns accuracy.

\subsection{The gate bounds the system}
End-to-end recall cannot exceed the gate's escalation recall, and that ceiling is
not uniform across pumps. Sweeping all 127 subsets of the seven deployable gate
features, the best achievable ceiling is \emph{flat} from three features upward
(0.865--0.873); what varies is the spread, with the best two-feature subset at
0.838 against a median of 0.467. Gate performance is therefore governed by which
features are chosen, not how many. Commissioning length binds only at seven
features, the first size at which a pump fails $n>10p$.

\textbf{The binding number is the worst machine, not the mean.} Across every
deployable configuration the best worst-machine ceiling is \textbf{0.52}, so the
two-tier system cannot exceed roughly 50\,\% recall on its worst pump however good
the gateway classifier is. We report the worst machine alongside the mean, and
the fault-count-pooled figure alongside both, because machines contributed
between 13 and 162 faulty windows each.

\section{The Operating Point as a Design Parameter}
\label{sec:opmethod}
The result in \S\ref{sec:op} is arithmetic applied to a design choice, so it
deserves scrutiny rather than celebration. We set out what it buys, what it does
not, and where it can mislead.

\subsection{What it buys}
\textbf{The promise is the invariant, not the false-alarm rate.} The obvious
objection is that we loosened a threshold until the number improved. We did not.
What an operator is offered --- at most one false alarm per pump per month --- is
identical at every row of Table~\ref{tab:cadence}; our implementation asserts this
invariant in a unit test. What changes is how many decisions that single tolerated
alarm is spread across. Spending it on 1080 hasty decisions rather than 30
considered ones is a choice, and it was costing a factor of 3.9 in recall.

\textbf{It is close to free.} The node's energy is dominated by continuous CUSUM
sensing, not by feature windows or radio, so cutting the decision rate by 36$\times$
leaves battery life unchanged at 1.2 years. And the fault classes routed through
this tier --- bearing wear, misalignment, impeller damage --- develop over weeks,
so a day of detection latency has no operational meaning.

\textbf{It relocates where effort is worth spending.} The cadence is worth
$3.9\times$ end-to-end recall; at the adopted cadence the spread between our three
non-trivial classifiers is $1.2\times$. More pointedly, the cadence determines the
\emph{sign} of the model comparison. A field that reports model rankings while
leaving the operating point implicit is not merely optimising the smaller term, it
is reporting a result that another operating point would reverse.

\subsection{A gate protocol, derived from getting it wrong twice}
\label{sec:protocol}
Two of the failures in this work were ours rather than the system's, and both were
protocol failures that produced plausible numbers. We set out what they cost and the
protocol we would now insist on, because the errors are not specific to us.

\textbf{Failure one: we mistook a broken commissioning procedure for a property of
industrial machinery.} We obtained a public dataset of three industrial centrifugal
pumps monitored continuously at 1\,Hz~\cite{cira2025} --- the only data available to
us with a real acquisition clock --- commissioned the gate on the earliest day and
ran it forward. It escalated 100\,\% of subsequent windows on all three pumps. We
wrote this up as evidence that plant demand moves the healthy baseline further than
any fault does. It is not. The commissioning day is 89\,\%, 92\,\% and 85\,\% idle:
the gate had learned a \emph{stopped} pump as normal and was correctly reporting that
a running pump does not look like it.

\textbf{Failure two: we reported a persistence result measured against that same
broken baseline}, and withdrew it (\S\ref{sec:persist}).

Neither error could have surfaced on our other datasets, and that is the
generalisable part. \textbf{ESPset and the rig data are implicitly run-state-gated by
whoever collected them}: every record is an acquisition a person chose to take on a
running machine. A monitor with no concept of ``off'' scores perfectly well on both
and learns an idle baseline the first time it is installed on a duty-cycled pump.

We would now require four things of any gate evaluation.

\emph{1. Establish run state before evaluating anything.} A condition monitor has
nothing to say about a stopped machine. Detection must be explicit --- a threshold
with hysteresis on a load-bearing channel --- and ``unknown'' must be representable,
because defaulting to ``running'' is how an idle baseline is learned. The gate must
also advance no internal state while off: the EWMA and CUSUM are recursive, so an
idle stretch drags their reference into the stopped-machine distribution and the
whole of the next running period escalates.

\emph{2. Count commissioning in observed running windows, not calendar time.} Our
requirement of $n > 10p$ healthy windows was checked against a nominal duty rather
than against what the machine actually did. One of the three pumps never accumulated
more than 54 running windows against the 60 its gate needed, and was silently
commissioned on a partial baseline. ``Not yet commissioned'' must be a reportable
state.

\emph{3. Report the operating point with every rate.} The same system reports 0.168
or 0.647 end-to-end recall depending on a decision cadence that used to be implicit
(\S\ref{sec:op}).

\emph{4. Do not claim a gate is validated on unlabelled data.} After correcting the
protocol, one pump gives a usable upper bound of 0.554 with stable vibration across
the record; one escalates 0.999 while its median vibration rises sevenfold, which is
either baseline drift or a developing bearing fault and no analysis of unlabelled
data decides which; one cannot be commissioned. \textbf{Unlabelled operational data
can falsify a gate but cannot validate one.} Our 100\,\% escalation rate proved our
protocol was broken --- that was real information. A low rate would have proved
nothing at all.

We report no escalation-rate claim from this dataset. What it establishes is the
protocol, and the protocol is the more transferable result.

\subsection{What it does not buy, and where it misleads}
\textbf{A naive version of this gains exactly nothing.} One might keep computing
36 windows a day and simply suppress duplicate alarms. That is not the same as
making fewer decisions: at a per-window rate of 0.00093 the probability of at least
one false alarm across 36 windows is $1-(1-0.00093)^{36} = 0.033$ per day, which is
0.99 per month --- precisely the budget already. Alarm suppression buys nothing
because the budget was never about how many alarms are \emph{emitted}; it is about
how many opportunities exist to emit one. The decisions must genuinely be fewer.

\textbf{Conflating two cadences costs a hundred days.} Sampling density serves two
unrelated purposes. It sets the false-alarm arithmetic, and it sets how quickly a
node accumulates the healthy windows its Mahalanobis gate needs before it can do
anything ($n > 10p$). Slowing both together would push commissioning of a
seven-feature gate from 2.9 days to 106. Our implementation separates them: the
node samples densely for the few days of a one-time commissioning phase and
sparsely thereafter. This is a schedule, not a capability, but it must be stated,
because the version of this result that slows everything uniformly is not
deployable.

\label{sec:persist}
\textbf{The better lever remains untested, and we now know what testing it needs.}
Requiring a fault to persist across $k$ of the last $n$ windows should dominate
sparse sampling, since it uses every window while still spending the budget once.
ESPset cannot test it: its records are independent measurements with no acquisition
clock, so any trailing-window rule there measures the order of a file, and an
earlier version of our analysis reported exactly such numbers before we withdrew
them. We then obtained plant telemetry that does have a clock and measured
persistence on it --- and had to withdraw that too, because the gate it was measured
against had been commissioned incorrectly (\S\ref{sec:protocol}). Evaluating
persistence needs a clock, labels, and a correctly commissioned baseline; we have
never had all three at once.

\textbf{Our cadence result is a deployment counterfactual, not a measurement at
that cadence.} For the same reason, we score one decision per record pooled across
folds and reason about what a daily cadence would imply. We have not run a node for
a month at one decision per day. The arithmetic linking budget to specificity is
exact; the assumption that a day's chosen window is as informative as the best of
36 is not tested.

\textbf{It applies only to slow faults.} Dry running is unaffected because it never
used this path --- it is a local trip at the node, decided in under a minute
against a contactor. A reader should not conclude that this system takes a day to
respond to a destroyed seal. The cadence governs the diagnostic tier alone.

\textbf{0.647 is still not a safety system.} Catching roughly two faults in three
at one false alarm per pump per month makes this a maintenance triage aid. It is a
defensible product claim and a large improvement on 0.168, and it is not a
replacement for inspection: a third of developing faults still reach failure
unflagged. We would rather state that than let a $3.9\times$ headline imply
otherwise.

\section{Limitations}
We list these in order of how much they threaten the headline claim, not in order
of how comfortable they are.

\textbf{The gate's escalation rate is measured on curated acquisitions.} The 0.059
underpinning the energy budget and the recall ceiling comes from datasets whose
every record is an acquisition somebody chose to take on a running machine. Our one
attempt to measure it on continuously monitored plant telemetry yielded a single
usable pump (\S\ref{sec:protocol}), which establishes nothing either way. Every
downstream number that depends on the gate --- uplinks per day, battery life, the
end-to-end recall in \S\ref{sec:op} --- carries that untested assumption.

\textbf{The cadence result is a deployment counterfactual.} We reason about what
one decision per runtime day implies; we have not run a node for a month at that
cadence. The arithmetic linking the alarm budget to per-decision specificity is
exact, but the assumption that a day's chosen window is as informative as the best
of thirty-six is untested, and the stronger lever --- persistence across $k$ of $n$
windows --- cannot be evaluated on data without acquisition timestamps
(\S\ref{sec:opmethod}).

\textbf{The mechanism of the normalisation effect is unresolved.} We report an effect, not an explanation.
Our own class-imbalance hypothesis is refuted by our own controlled test
(\S\ref{sec:norm}), and the surviving account is only partially supported. A
reader should treat the practical recommendation --- pool the training machines
--- as empirical, and should not assume it transfers to feature spaces or machine
types unlike those tested here.

\textbf{The cross-machine claim rests on one dataset.} ESPset is the only public
source with a genuine cross-machine axis for pumps. Our Paderborn check confirms
the effect appears on independent data, but its held-out unit is a bearing on a
shared test rig, not a machine with its own installation and duty cycle. It
validates the phenomenon, not the pump-specific magnitude.

\textbf{The effect size is not stable.} Across feature sets on the same pumps the
gap ranges from $+0.078$ to $+0.265$. The direction is invariant; the magnitude is
not, and we would not defend any single number as the effect size.

\textbf{Machine count is binding, and everything else is already past
usefulness.} Eleven machines is the largest cross-machine axis in public pump data
and is not enough for the per-machine confidence intervals to separate: seed noise
is roughly 0.016 while the per-machine bootstrap intervals span roughly 0.19. A
twelfth pump would sharpen this far more than a sixth seed. Independently,
Vieira \emph{et al.}~\cite{vieira2025} find the number of unique training units
decisive for robust performance, which suggests this is a property of the problem
rather than of our setup.

\textbf{The Paderborn folds required a protocol we had to design.} Plain
leave-one-bearing-out is degenerate there, because each bearing carries one damage
state, so a single-unit holdout yields a single-class test set and every model
scores zero. Holding out one bearing per class per fold fixes this, but it is our
construction and yields only three folds.

\textbf{The alarm budget bounds deployability.} Roughly 20\,\% recall at one false
alarm per pump per month makes this a triage aid, not a replacement for
inspection. This, rather than macro-F1, is the number that says what the system is
for.

\textbf{The gate bounds the system before the classifier does.} Across every
deployable gate configuration the best worst-machine recall ceiling is 0.52, so
end-to-end recall on that pump cannot exceed roughly half however good the
gateway model.

\textbf{ESPset is offshore submersibles}, not smallholder irrigation pumps. They
share failure physics and little else. \textbf{Our generic features underperform
expert-designed ones}: no subset of what our extractor computes reaches the
ceiling of the dataset's own published columns. \textbf{No run-to-failure data},
so we make no remaining-useful-life claim. \textbf{No collected rig data}: the
acquisition path is implemented and exercisable against a simulated backend, but
no real acquisition was performed and we claim no contribution from it.

\section{Conclusion}
Two decisions usually left implicit each outweighed our choice of classifier.

How often the system decides was worth $3.9\times$ end-to-end recall
($0.168 \rightarrow 0.647$), holding the operator-facing alarm promise fixed and
costing nothing in energy or in meaningful latency --- and it reversed which of our
models was best. How the features are normalised
was worth 0.20--0.28 macro-F1 across eleven in-service pumps, where the gap between
our best and worst model on the same data was 0.072. The difference between
classifiers, which is what the field's effort mostly goes into, was the smallest
term in our own system.

Neither finding is comfortable. The cadence result is a deployment counterfactual
rather than a month of field operation, and the stronger version of it --- requiring
faults to persist across windows --- is untestable on data with no acquisition
timestamps. The normalisation result replicates on an independent benchmark but we
could not explain it, and we refute our own first explanation rather than publish
it. We report both because an effect with an honest ``mechanism unknown'' is worth
more than a tidy story we cannot support.

The practical consequence is narrow and concrete. Report the decision cadence and
the normalisation strategy alongside every cross-machine result: two systems using
different ones are not comparable, and the difference will usually exceed whatever
modelling contribution is being claimed.

The architectural results are corrections of our own assumptions. Dry running
belongs at the node and to the current channel rather than the vibration channel.
Transmission is not the node's energy problem; continuous sensing is. And the gate,
not the classifier, bounds what the system can detect.
